1 Welford's recurrence formulas

1.1 Derivation

Let x1, …, xk be the data seen so far, Mk their mean and SSk = Σi≤k (xi − Mk)² the sum of squared deviations. The aim is to update both when one new value xk arrives, without storing the earlier values.

Mean (Knuth). From kMk = (k−1)Mk−1 + xk we get

Mk = Mk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)/k, with M0 = 0.

Write δ = xk − Mk−1, the surprise of the new value. Then Mk = Mk−1 + δ/k, and also xk − Mk = δ − δ/k = δ(k−1)/k.

Sum of squares. Split SSk into the first k−1 terms and the last one, and centre the first terms on the old mean:

SSk = Σi<k (xi − Mk−1 − δ/k)² + (xk − Mk)²

= SSk−1 − (2δ/k) Σi<k (xi − Mk−1) + (k−1)δ²/k² + δ²(k−1)²/k².

The middle sum is zero because deviations from a mean add up to zero. The last two terms combine to δ²(k−1)/k² · (1 + (k−1)) = δ²(k−1)/k. Therefore

SSk = SSk−1 + δ²(k−1)/k = SSk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)(xk − Mk), with SS0 = 0.

The second form follows because (xk − Mk) = δ(k−1)/k. It is the one used in code: it needs only the old mean and the new mean, and both factors are small numbers when the data are stable, which is the source of the numerical advantage.

Variance. The sample variance and the population variance are sk² = SSk/(k−1) for k ≥ 2 and σ̂k² = SSk/k. They are computed by one division after the last update, or at any step when a running estimate is wanted.

1.2 Code

let k = 0, mean = 0, ss = 0;
for (const x of data) {
  k++;  const d = x - mean;  mean += d / k;  ss += d * (x - mean);   // the three lines
}
const sampleVar = ss / (k - 1);       // unbiased, needs k >= 2
# Python version
k = mean = ss = 0
for x in data:
    k += 1; d = x - mean; mean += d / k; ss += d * (x - mean)
sample_var = ss / (k - 1)

Worked example with the data 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9 (computed live by the page):

After eight values the mean is 5, SS = 32, the sample variance is 32/7 = 4.5714 and the population variance is 32/8 = 4. These agree with the direct two-pass computation.

1.3 Numerical behaviour

The textbook one-pass shortcut computes (Σx² − (Σx)²/n)/(n−1). It subtracts two huge nearly equal numbers when the data have a large mean and a small spread. Test: the values 109 + {4, 7, 13, 16} (true sample variance 30) and 100000 scores of the Binomial distribution of chapter 2 shifted by 109 (true variance about 2.1).

On the 5000 unshifted scores of a separate check, Welford and the two-pass method differ by 5.8·10−15 in the variance and 1.0·10−14 in the mean, i.e. by rounding only. The shortcut formula, on the other hand, returns a negative variance in both shifted tests, which is impossible for a variance. Welford's method needs one pass, constant memory, and stays accurate; this is why it is the standard way to compute running statistics over data streams (logs, packet counters).

2 Sampling mean and variance of penetration scores

2.1 Setup

This extends Homework 3: every penetration attempt now receives a score between 0 and 10. Two fixed distributions are used.

The main case is Binomial(10, 0.3): ten independent checks, each succeeding with probability 0.3 (the p of the previous homework), with μ = 3 and σ² = 2.1. The second distribution is the uniform one on {0, …, 10}, with μ = 5 and σ² = 10, which has a very different shape (excess kurtosis −1.22) and shows that the conclusions do not depend on one distribution. There are m = 2000 hackers, and each attacks n systems and receives n scores. The attack uses the recurrence of chapter 1 to compute, for each hacker, the sampling mean x̄ and the sampling variance s² (divisor n−1). Then the two sets of m values are averaged and compared with μ and σ². The experiment is repeated for n = 2, 5, 10, 30, 100, 1000, with a fixed seed (2026) so the numbers can be reproduced. The graphs show the running estimates of five hackers as they attack 200 systems, one score at a time.

2.3 Comparison with μ and σ²

The average of the m sampling means is very close to μ for all n: for the Binomial it is 3.0230, 2.9955, 2.9946, 3.0036, 3.0000, 3.0006 (μ = 3) and for the Uniform 5.0890, 4.9558, 4.9922, 4.9927, 4.9973, 5.0001 (μ = 5). The average of the sampling variances is equally close to σ², even at n = 2: 2.1020, 2.0691, 2.0774, 2.1023, 2.0957, 2.1036 (σ² = 2.1) and 9.6735, 9.8482, 9.9552, 10.0185, 9.9889, 9.9965 (σ² = 10). The deviations are of the size of the Monte Carlo error, which falls with m. Hence both estimators are unbiased: E[x̄] = μ and E[s²] = σ² for every n, not only for large n.

The divisor matters. The columns for the biased variance SS/n show the systematic underestimate by the factor (n−1)/n: for the Binomial 1.0510 (n = 2, expected 1.05), 1.6553 (n = 5, expected 1.68), 1.8697 (n = 10, expected 1.89), against σ² = 2.1. The bias disappears slowly: 2.0747 at n = 100 and 2.1015 at n = 1000. Dividing by n−1 corrects exactly this: the sample's deviations are measured from x̄, which is fitted to the same data, so they are slightly too small, and one degree of freedom is lost.

2.4 Why this is useful in inferential statistics

In practice we see only one sample of n scores and do not know μ or σ². The experiment shows what this one sample is worth: x̄ and s² are right on average, so the estimates are not systematically off, and the spread across hackers (the standard error) tells how far a single estimate may be from the truth. The standard error σ/√n can be estimated from the sample itself by s/√n, which gives a confidence interval x̄ ± t·s/√n. The last two columns of each results table test this: the share of hackers whose interval contains μ is 0.9510, 0.9515, 0.9490, 0.9500, 0.9580 (n = 5 to 1000) for the Binomial with t quantiles, with the nominal level 0.95. Using the normal value 1.96 instead of t under-covers for small n (0.6715 at n = 2, 0.8625 at n = 5, 0.9180 at n = 10), because s itself is uncertain. At n = 2 the t interval also falls short (0.8060), since the Binomial is discrete: many hackers get two equal scores, so s = 0 and the interval collapses to a point. Welford's formulas matter here for a practical reason: they let a monitoring system produce x̄ and s² for each attacker over a long stream of events without storing it.

2.5 Effect of the number of systems n on the variability

Distribution: n for the histograms:

The first histogram is the standardized sample mean (x̄ − μ)/(σ/√n) of the 2000 hackers against the standard normal density; the second is the sample variance with a normal curve of the theoretical mean and standard deviation; the third plots the standard deviations of x̄ and s² against n on log-log axes (solid: observed, dashed: theory).

The variability of the sampling means decreases like 1/√n: the observed standard deviation of x̄ for the Binomial is 1.0244, 0.6351, 0.4513, 0.2676, 0.1447, 0.0453 against the theoretical σ/√n = 1.0247, 0.6481, 0.4583, 0.2646, 0.1449, 0.0458. Between n = 2 and n = 1000 it falls by a factor of 22.6, close to √500 = 22.4. The variability of the sampling variances also falls, from 2.9044 to 0.0915 (theory 2.9235 to 0.0910), a factor of about 32. Its exact theoretical value is √[(μ4 − σ4(n−3)/(n−1))/n], with μ4 the fourth central moment (12.684 for the Binomial, 178 for the Uniform), so it depends on the shape of the distribution through the kurtosis, which the mean does not. The Uniform shows the same pattern: sd(x̄) 2.2236 to 0.0998 and sd(s²) 11.5779 to 0.2740. The sampling distribution of x̄ is skewed and spiky for n = 2 and close to normal from about n = 30; the sampling distribution of s² takes longer to become symmetric, which fits its slower convergence. In both cases, a larger n means that each hacker's estimates are closer to μ and σ² and that hackers resemble each other more; this is the law of large numbers for the mean and the variance, and the 1/√n rate is the central limit theorem scale of Homework 3.

4 Conclusions

Welford's recurrence gives the mean, the sum of squares and the variance in one pass with three lines of code, and it stays accurate on data where the textbook shortcut fails. Applied to m hackers, the average of the sampling means is μ and the average of the sampling variances is σ² for every n, which is unbiasedness; dividing by n instead of n−1 leaves a bias of σ²/n. The spread of the estimates around the truth falls like 1/√n, at a rate that depends on the kurtosis for the variance. These facts underlie confidence intervals, whose observed coverage reaches the nominal 95% as soon as the t quantile is used and n is moderate.
