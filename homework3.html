Homework 3: Penetration scores, LLN and CLT

Petar Rakočević, Cybersecurity MSc, Statistics 2026/27

1 Model

We have n systems and m attackers. Each attacker works through the systems one after another and receives a score Xi for system i. The scores are independent and identically distributed, and attackers are independent of each other, so each attacker produces its own trajectory Sk = X1 + … + Xk, k ≤ n. Two score models are used.

BernoulliRademacher
ValuesX ∈ {0, 1}, P(X=1) = pX ∈ {−1, +1}, each with probability 1/2
Meaning1 = system penetrated, 0 = not+1 = gain for the attacker, −1 = gain for the defender

The three quantities studied are the total score Sn, the average score Sn/n and the normalized total Sn/√n. From independence: E[Sn] = nμ and Var(Sn) = nσ², hence Var(Sn/n) = σ²/n and Var(Sn/√n) = σ². The theory predicts three different behaviours, one for each scaling: the total spreads out like √n, the average collapses onto μ, and only the √n scaling keeps a constant spread. The simulation checks all three.

2 Simulation design

For each model and each n in {10, 100, 1000, 10000} we generate m = 5000 independent copies of Sn. Bernoulli uses p = 0.3. The generator is a seeded Mulberry32 (seed 2026), so every number on this page can be reproduced. The deviation threshold of the LLN test is ε = 0.05. To compare with the standard normal we use the standardized value T = (Sn − nμ)/(σ√n); in the Rademacher case μ = 0, σ = 1, so T is exactly Sn/√n. Distances to the normal are measured with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic.

function sims(model,p,n,m,u){const S=new Float64Array(m);
  for(let j=0;j<m;j++){let s=0;
    for(let i=0;i<n;i++) s += model==='bern' ? (u()<p?1:0) : (u()<0.5?1:-1);
    S[j]=s}
  return S}               // then A=S/n, Z=S/Math.sqrt(n), T=(S-n*mu)/(sd*Math.sqrt(n))

5 Law of large numbers: convergence in probability of Sn/n

The claim is that P(|Sn/n − μ| > ε) → 0 for every ε > 0. Chebyshev gives the bound σ²/(nε²), and Hoeffding gives a much sharper exponential one.

For Bernoulli, the observed deviation probability is 0.7216, 0.2346, 0.0004 and 0 for n = 10, 100, 1000, 10000. Chebyshev only gives 1, 0.84, 0.084, 0.0084, so it is valid but conservative; Hoeffding's 2exp(−2nε²) gives 0.0135 at n = 1000 and is much closer to the truth. For Rademacher the convergence is slower for the same ε (0.7554, 0.6204, 0.1064, 0) because the score range is two instead of one and the variance is larger (1 against 0.21). The 5%-95% band of Sn/n shrinks around μ: for Bernoulli its width is 0.400, 0.140, 0.048, 0.0149, a factor of about 2.9-3.2 per tenfold increase of n, in line with 1/√n (√10 ≈ 3.16). The figures show the band closing on the mean (left Bernoulli, right Rademacher).

6 Variance under the three normalizations

Log-log plots of the three sample variances against n (left Bernoulli, right Rademacher). Var(Sn) is a line of slope +1, Var(Sn/n) a line of slope −1, and Var(Sn/√n) is horizontal. This is the content of the normalization: dividing by n is too much (the distribution degenerates at μ, which is the LLN), no division at all is too little (the distribution spreads without limit), and √n is the unique scale at which the limit is non-degenerate. For Bernoulli, Sn/√n has a constant variance 0.21 but its mean √n·p grows (0.954, 3.00, 9.49, 30.0), so the quantity itself does not converge; only the centred version does. In the symmetric case μ = 0 and the mean is zero for all n, which is why Rademacher is the clean case for the CLT.

7 Central limit theorem: Sn/√n → N(0,1) in distribution

Model: n:

The histogram is the density of the standardized value and the gold curve is the standard normal density. The shape is already visibly bell-like at n = 100, and indistinguishable by eye at n = 1000. Numerically, the KS distance of the Rademacher case is 0.1302, 0.0530, 0.0224, 0.0121. Two effects limit how low it can go: the sampling error of a KS statistic from m = 5000 samples is about 0.87/√m = 0.0123, and a lattice effect, since Sn/√n takes only values with spacing 2/√n, which forces a distance of about 0.40/√n (0.1265, 0.0400, 0.0126, 0.0040). At n = 10 the whole distance is the lattice effect, and at n = 10000 the value is at the sampling noise level. The coverage of the interval [−1.96, 1.96] is 0.981, 0.945, 0.948, 0.951, close to the nominal 0.95 from n = 100. Excess kurtosis is −0.278, −0.098, 0.020, 0.017 (theory −2/n: −0.2, −0.02, −0.002, −0.0002), with a sampling error of about 0.07; skewness is within noise of 0. For Bernoulli the standardized skewness decays like (1−2p)/√(np(1−p)), predicted 0.276 and 0.087 at n = 10 and 100, observed 0.268 and 0.075; the Bernoulli KS values are 0.1498, 0.0486, 0.0217, 0.0131, slightly above the Rademacher ones because of that remaining skew.

8 Choice of m

n controls how close the distribution is to its limit; m controls how well we can measure it. The table shows the average KS distance for Rademacher at fixed n = 1000 as m grows (with repeated runs for small m to smooth the result).

The distance falls like 1/√m (0.118 at m = 50, 0.040 at m = 1000, 0.016 at m = 20000) until it hits the lattice floor at 0.0126, which is why a larger m cannot reveal anything beyond that. To resolve a KS difference of 0.01 one needs m of order (0.87/0.01)² ≈ 7500 trajectories, and m = 5000 is therefore a reasonable choice for the main tables. A small m, such as 50, makes a perfectly normal distribution look 'non-normal' by 0.12.

9 What this says about n systems and m attackers

One attacker, many systems

The total score Sn is the number of penetrated systems (Bernoulli) or the net outcome (Rademacher). It grows with n, and its uncertainty grows as √n. The penetration rate Sn/n becomes predictable as the network gets larger: with p = 0.3, the 5-95% range of the rate is 0.10-0.50 for 10 systems and 0.2926-0.3075 for 10000. A large organisation therefore sees almost the expected breach rate every time, while a small one can be very lucky or very unlucky. In the symmetric model, the net score of an attacker has typical size √n, so 1000 systems give a typical swing of about 32.

Many attackers

The aggregate average over all n·m attempts has standard deviation σ/√(nm), so for the average only the product nm matters. The table compares the theory with the empirical value from blocks of m independent trajectories of the same simulation (Bernoulli, p = 0.3):

Theory and simulation agree within a few percent; the m = 50 rows use only 100 blocks, so their sampling error is around 7%. The distribution of the individual attacker is a different matter. The risk that at least one of m attackers reaches a high score grows with m. For n = 100 systems, the chance that one attacker penetrates at least 40 (expected 30, standard deviation 4.58) is 0.0210 exactly (0.0178 in the simulation). The chance that at least one of m attackers does so:

With 100 attackers the probability is 0.88, and with 500 it is almost certain, although each attacker is individually unlikely to reach the threshold. For the Rademacher model the best of m attackers reaches a score around σ√n·√(2 ln m) for large m, which grows with both parameters. So: larger n makes the average score more predictable (LLN) but the total more spread out (CLT scale √n); larger m makes the aggregate average more predictable by the factor 1/√m, but the extreme outcome among attackers worse. A defender who looks at average rates sees stability, while one who looks at the worst attacker sees the opposite. The model assumes independent, identical attackers and systems; real networks have dependent and unequal systems, so these results are the baseline, not a prediction.

11 Conclusions

All three theoretical predictions are confirmed numerically. The average Sn/n converges in probability to μ, with a spread that falls like 1/√n. The variance of the total grows like n, the variance of the average falls like 1/n, and Sn/√n holds its variance at σ². For the symmetric Rademacher case the normalized total converges in distribution to N(0,1), with a KS distance that reaches the level set by sampling noise and by the lattice effect. The number of attackers m does not change the distribution of an individual trajectory, but it sets the accuracy of any estimate based on the pooled data, and it governs the extreme outcome among attackers.
