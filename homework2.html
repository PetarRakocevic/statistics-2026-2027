1 The toy elliptic-curve scheme

Part 1 gave us the one-way map k → Q = kG on secp256k1. A map like that does not encrypt anything on its own, so to encrypt a message I use it as a key-agreement step (Diffie-Hellman on the curve) and build the rest in the style of ECIES. The restriction that makes the experiments feasible is that the private key k is a small integer (at most 2b for a chosen b, here up to 22 bits). The curve and G are the real secp256k1 ones.

StepWhoOperation
Key pairReceiverprivate k (small), public Q = kG
EncryptSenderpick ephemeral r; send R = rG; shared point S = rQ; keystream = SHA-256(S.x ‖ counter); ciphertext = message XOR keystream
DecryptReceiverS = kR (equals rQ because rkG = krG); regenerate keystream; XOR again

Role of the keys. k is the secret only the receiver holds. Q is public and lets anyone start a conversation. r is a fresh one-time secret that makes every message use a different keystream, and R is its public image, sent with the ciphertext. The curve provides only the shared secret S; the actual hiding is done by XOR with a hash-derived keystream, which is also how real hybrid schemes work.

2 The Caesar cipher

Each letter is moved by a fixed shift s: E(x) = (x + s) mod 26, D(y) = (y − s) mod 26, with non-letters unchanged. The key is the single number s, so the key space has 26 values (25 that change the text). Despite its simplicity, it is the model of every monoalphabetic cipher: each plaintext letter always maps to the same ciphertext letter.

The common point is that both are ciphers whose output is a deterministic function of message and key, and both fail completely if the key space can be enumerated. The difference is where the secrecy lives: in Caesar it is the shift itself, in the EC scheme it rests on the hardness of finding k or r from Q or R, and on the strength of the hash.

6 Statistical cryptanalysis of the Caesar cipher

Idea. For every candidate shift s, undo it and measure how English the result looks. The reference distribution is the standard English letter frequency table pi (a-z). The score is the chi-square statistic χ² = Σ (Oi − n·pi)² / (n·pi), where Oi counts letter i in the decrypted candidate. The shift with the smallest χ² is the estimated key. 7 Effect of length and comparison of methods

Corpus (the plain text source of the test; replace it with any English text, the longer the better):

Trials per length:

Four methods are compared. Naive E assumes the most frequent ciphertext letter is e. Dot product picks the shift maximising Σ Oi·pi. Chi-square as in chapter 6. N-gram scores each of the 26 decrypted candidates by how many common English bigrams and trigrams (th, he, in, the, and, ing, …) it contains. Exhaustive key search is the common skeleton of all four: they all try all 26 shifts and differ only in the scoring function; a human reading the 26 candidates is a fifth, scoring-free variant, which is trivial for 26 keys.

Discussion

Success rises with length because the observed histogram converges to the language distribution (law of large numbers), and the 26 candidate scores separate. For a handful of letters all methods are close to guessing, since few letters cannot reveal a distribution; some words, such as "zzz" or "eee", are in fact ambiguous. Naive E is the weakest because it relies on a single letter, which is often beaten by t, a or o in short texts. Chi-square and the dot product use all 26 letters and behave similarly. N-gram scoring uses structure instead of single-letter counts and is usually the most robust on short text. Limits shared by all: they need a known language and enough text, they assume a monoalphabetic text without noise, and numbers or unusual vocabulary shift the statistics.

8 Attacking the toy EC scheme

The restriction k ≤ 2b allows two attacks, both working from the public key Q.

Key bits b: 16

Exhaustive search: compute G, 2G, 3G, … until kG equals Q; at most 2b additions. Baby-step giant-step: with m = ⌈√(2b)⌉, store jG for j < m in a table, then walk Q − i·mG for i = 0, 1, … until it hits the table; then k = i·m + j. It needs about 2·√(2b) operations at the cost of √(2b) memory. Once k is known, S = kR and the message is decrypted. Exhaustive search is skipped above 17 bits in the demo to keep the page responsive.

Difference from the Caesar attack

The Caesar attack needs language statistics as the judge of a candidate key, so its answer is probabilistic and depends on text length. The EC attack has an exact judge, Q = kG, so it needs no statistics and never fails for any message length; it can even be run without knowing the plaintext's language. Without Q, one can still try each k on the ciphertext and recognise printable text, but the public key gives a cleaner test.

9 Why a properly configured ECC scheme is not attackable this way

Exhaustive search over n ≈ 1.16·1077 ≈ 2256 keys is out of reach: even at a billion additions per second it would take about 1060 years. Baby-step giant-step would need roughly 2128 table entries, far beyond any storage. The best generic attack on the curve is Pollard's rho, about 2128 operations with negligible memory, which is still infeasible (the demo estimates it from the measured speed). Hence the doubling of the bit-length: security level is about half the key size in bits. In addition, no structural weakness is known for secp256k1 itself (it is not a supersingular or anomalous curve), and the key space only protects if k is chosen uniformly at random and not from a weak source.

10 Weaknesses: key space versus design

Caused by the restricted key space: exhaustive search and BSGS; also the ephemeral r, if small, can be recovered from R = rG the same way, giving S = rQ without touching k.

Caused by the design of the toy scheme itself, even with a full key space: (1) no authentication: XOR is malleable, flipping a ciphertext bit flips the same plaintext bit, and the receiver cannot notice; real schemes add a MAC or use an AEAD cipher. (2) Ephemeral reuse: the same r twice gives the same keystream, so C₁ ⊕ C₂ = M₁ ⊕ M₂, a two-time pad. (3) Home-made key derivation: SHA-256(x ‖ counter) is acceptable for a demo but a real scheme uses a proper KDF with context. (4) Poor randomness: if k or r come from a weak generator the full key space is irrelevant. (5) No validation that R lies on the curve, which in some implementations allows invalid-curve attacks. (6) Plaintext length is revealed, and the receiver's identity is bound only by Q.

Conclusion: the Caesar cipher falls to statistics because its structure leaks into the ciphertext; the toy EC scheme falls to brute force only because I deliberately shrank k. With a 256-bit key the weaknesses that remain are the ones in the design, not in the curve.
