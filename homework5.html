1 Riemann and Lebesgue integration

1.1 Two definitions

Riemann. Cut the domain [a, b] into N subintervals of width h = (b − a)/N, pick a tag ξi in each, and add the areas of the strips: SN = Σ f(ξi)·h. The function is Riemann integrable when the upper Darboux sum (sup of f on each strip) and the lower sum (inf on each strip) approach the same number as h → 0; then every choice of tags gives that number. With ξi the left end of the strip this is the left sum, with the centre it is the midpoint sum.

Lebesgue. Cut the range of f instead: levels lo = t0 < t1 < … < tN = hi. Each band of values has a preimage Ej = f−1([tj, tj+1)), a measurable subset of the domain that can be scattered over many pieces. The lower and upper sums are Σ tj·μ(Ej) and Σ tj+1·μ(Ej), the integrals of simple functions below and above f. Summing by parts gives the form I actually compute, the layer-cake formula

∫ f dμ = lo·|D| + ∫lohi μ{x : f(x) > t} dt

so the Lebesgue integral is a one-dimensional integral over the range of the measure of the superlevel sets. The usual comparison is counting money: Riemann adds the coins in the order they come out of the pocket, Lebesgue first sorts them by value and multiplies each value by the size of its pile. For a random variable X ≥ 0 the same formula reads E[X] = ∫0∞ P(X > t) dt, which is why the Lebesgue view is the natural one in probability.

1.2 Algorithms

The Riemann sums need only N evaluations. The Lebesgue sums need the measure μ{f > t} for every level t, and that is where the work is. I implemented two ways of getting it.

Preimage by root finding (L-low, L-up, L-mid). f is evaluated once on a grid of G = 2000 cells. For a level t every cell is either entirely above t, entirely below, or contains a crossing; crossings are located by bisection to about 10−15. The preimage {f > t} is then a union of intervals whose total length is the measure. L-low uses the upper end of each level band, L-up the lower end, and L-mid the centre tj + h/2, the range analogue of the midpoint rule.

Preimage from sorted samples (L-sample). f is evaluated at K = 100 000 midpoints of the domain, the values are sorted once, and μ{f > t} is the share of values above t (binary search) times |D|. This is the empirical distribution of f(U) for U uniform on the domain.

For the unbounded 1/√x the range is cut at a level T. The tail lost above T is ∫T∞ t−2 dt = 1/T, and the midpoint error of the levels is of order h² = (T/N)². Balancing 1/T = T²/N² gives T = N2/3, which I use.

function riemann(F,N,tag){const h=(F.b-F.a)/N;let s=0;
  for(let i=0;i<N;i++) s+=F.f(F.a+(i+tag)*h);           // tag 0 = left, 0.5 = midpoint
  return s*h}
function meas(P,t){let m=0;                                // P = grid X[0..G], Y = f(X)
  for(let i=0;i<G;i++){const u=Y[i]>t, v=Y[i+1]>t;
    if(u&&v) m+=X[i+1]-X[i];                            // whole cell above the level
    else if(u!==v){ /* bisection for the crossing z */ m+= u ? z-X[i] : X[i+1]-z }}
  return m}
function lebesgue(F,N){const h=(hi-lo)/N;let s=0;          // layer cake, midpoint levels
  for(let j=0;j<N;j++) s+=h*meas(P, lo+(j+0.5)*h);
  return lo*(F.b-F.a)+s}

1.4 Accuracy at N = 1000

Signed errors (estimate − exact value) with N = 1000 strips for Riemann and N = 1000 levels for Lebesgue:

For the smooth functions the Riemann midpoint sum is the most accurate: 8.22·10−7 for sin(x), 8.33·10−8 for x² and 1.31·10−7 for sin(x)/x, against 5.36·10−6, 1.90·10−6 and 4.59·10−6 for L-mid. The left sum and L-low make errors of the same size, about 1.6·10−3 for sin(x), because both are first-order rules, one in x and one in t. L-low and L-up always bracket the exact value from below and above (they are integrals of simple functions under and over f), which a Riemann sum with arbitrary tags does not guarantee. For the two step functions every method is exact up to rounding (around 10−16) at this N, and for |x − 0.5| as well: in that case μ{f > t} = 1 − 2t is linear, so the midpoint rule in t is exact, and the kink at 0.5 is a grid point, so the midpoint rule in x is exact too.

1.5 Convergence

Function:

Observed order p = log10(|error at N = 1000| / |error at N = 10000|), i.e. the slope of the lines in the plot:

The Riemann sums show the textbook orders: 1 for the left sum and 2 for the midpoint sum on smooth functions. The Lebesgue midpoint sum has order 1.5 on sin(x), x² and sin(x)/x (errors 4.63·10−3, 1.64·10−4, 5.36·10−6, 1.71·10−7 for sin). The reason is the shape of the distribution function. For x² on [0, 1], μ{x² > t} = 1 − √t, whose derivative is infinite at t = 0; for sin(x), μ{sin x > t} = π − 2 arcsin t, infinite derivative at t = 1. The midpoint rule in t loses half an order on a square-root singularity. In general every point where f′ = 0 (a flat maximum or minimum) produces such a singularity, because a whole neighbourhood of x maps into a very thin band of values. So the two approaches have mirror-image weaknesses: Riemann accuracy depends on how smooth f is as a function of x, Lebesgue accuracy on how smooth its distribution function is as a function of t, and flat parts of f are exactly where the second one is rough.

For 1/√x the picture turns around. The left sum is infinite for every N (the first tag is 0). The midpoint sum converges with order 0.5: 1.91·10−1, 6.05·10−2, 1.91·10−2, 6.05·10−3. The Lebesgue sum with the cut T = N2/3 reaches 2.31·10−1, 4.39·10−2, 1.08·10−2, 1.88·10−3, an average order of 0.70 and an error three times smaller at N = 10000. Cutting the range handles the singularity directly, since the function is only large on a set of tiny measure, while a domain partition keeps sampling close to the pole.

1.6 Computational efficiency

Function evaluations and run time at N = 10000 (time in milliseconds, measured in the browser engine; the re-run button in chapter 3 measures it again on your machine):

A Riemann sum costs exactly N evaluations and well under a millisecond. The root-finding Lebesgue sum costs the grid (2001 evaluations) plus about 55 evaluations per crossing per level: 1.64 million for sin(x), which crosses each level twice, and 0.78 million for the monotone functions. It ran 120-175 ms for the regular functions and 1.17 s for sin(1/x), where each level is crossed many times. For the same accuracy the gap is even larger: the midpoint sum reaches 10−7 on sin(x) with about 3000 evaluations, while L-mid needs 10000 levels and 1.6 million evaluations for 1.7·10−7.

L-sample is cheaper (100 000 evaluations plus one sort, 3-10 ms) and it shows where the two definitions meet. Adding sorted values over fine levels gives the mean of the samples times |D|, which is exactly the midpoint Riemann sum with K points, only summed in a different order. Any numerical measure of a preimage has to be read from the domain, so in practice the Lebesgue method is a Riemann sampling scheme followed by a sort. Its advantage is theoretical (limit theorems, unbounded functions, abstract spaces), not speed.

1.7 Where the two approaches behave differently

Jumps that do not sit on the grid. The step function 1/3 is integrated exactly by every Riemann sum in chapter 1.4 only because 0.5 is a grid point when N is even. With odd N the jump falls inside a strip:

The Riemann error is the jump times the misplaced part of one strip, 1/N here (0.0909 at N = 11, 0.0099 at N = 101, 0.000999 at N = 1001). The Lebesgue midpoint sum is 2 up to rounding for every N, because the range has only two values: every level between 1 and 3 has the same preimage [0.5, 1], whose end the bisection locates to 10−15. For functions with few values the Lebesgue partition is the natural one; this is also the reason simple functions are its building blocks.

Infinitely many oscillations. sin(1/x) is bounded and has a single discontinuity, so it is Riemann integrable, with ∫01 sin(1/x) dx = sin 1 − Ci(1) ≈ 0.504067. The midpoint sum converges slowly (errors 1.30·10−1, −1.58·10−2, −1.32·10−3, −2.96·10−4). The root-finding Lebesgue sum stops improving at about −1.7·10−3, because near 0 each preimage {sin(1/x) > t} has infinitely many components. The period of the oscillation near x is about 2πx², shorter than the grid cell of 5·10−4 for x below roughly 0.009, so crossings in that region are invisible to the grid and adding levels does not help. L-sample, which uses 50 times more domain points, reaches 4.3·10−5. Again, the Lebesgue sum is only as good as its view of the domain.

1.8 Pathological functions

A remark on floating point first. Every double-precision number is a fraction p/2k, so it is rational. A function that tests "is x rational" cannot be evaluated on doubles at all: it would return 1 everywhere. I therefore represent tags symbolically: a rational tag is the pair (i, N) for i/N, and an irrational tag is marked as such, for example (i + 1/√2)/N.

Dirichlet function f = 1 on ℚ, 0 elsewhere. Every strip of every partition contains rationals and irrationals, so the upper Darboux sum is 1 and the lower is 0 for every N. The Riemann sum with rational tags i/N is 1, with irrational tags 0, and no refinement brings them together: f is not Riemann integrable. For Lebesgue there are only two values and two preimages, ℚ∩[0, 1] and the irrationals. The rationals are countable: enumerate them as r1, r2, … and cover rk with an interval of length ε·2−k; the total length is at most ε, for any ε, so μ(ℚ∩[0, 1]) = 0. Numerically, with all rationals of denominator up to Q and ε = 0.01:

The number of covered rationals grows like 3Q²/π² (33, 3045, 304 193), while the covered length stays at 0.01. Hence ∫ f dμ = 1·μ(ℚ) + 0·μ(irrationals) = 0. A Riemann sum with tags drawn from a continuous random distribution also gives 0, since such a tag is irrational with probability 1; random tags compute the Lebesgue value.

Thomae's function f(p/q) = 1/q in lowest terms, f = 0 on irrationals. It is discontinuous at every rational and continuous at every irrational; its discontinuities form a countable, hence null, set, so by Lebesgue's criterion it is Riemann integrable, with integral 0. With rational tags i/N the value is f(i/N) = gcd(i, N)/N, so the left sum is exactly (1/N²)·Σ gcd(i, N). The upper Darboux sum uses on each strip the fraction with the smallest denominator q inside it, sup = 1/q. The lower sum is always 0.

The left sums go to 0, but how fast depends on the arithmetic of N: for a prime N they equal (2N − 1)/N² ≈ 2/N (0.0205 at N = 97, 0.00201 at N = 997, 0.000201 at N = 9973), while N = 10000, with many divisors, gives 0.00126, six times more. The upper Darboux sum falls slowly, 0.457, 0.131, 0.0374, 0.0111 for N = 10 to 10000, roughly like N−0.53, but it does fall to 0, which confirms Riemann integrability. The Lebesgue computation needs no limit: for any level t > 0 the set {f > t} = {p/q : q < 1/t} is finite, so it has measure 0, every term of the layer-cake sum is 0, and the result is exactly 0 for any number of levels. 1.9 Discussion

Do the two approaches converge to the same value? Yes, for every Riemann-integrable function in the list. The estimates converge to the exact integrals in all cases where the method can see the function (sin, x², the steps, |x − 0.5|, sin(x)/x, and for 1/√x, as an improper Riemann integral, both give 2). This agrees with the theorem that a bounded Riemann-integrable function on [a, b] is Lebesgue integrable with the same integral. The converse fails: the Dirichlet function has a Lebesgue integral (0) and no Riemann integral.

Accuracy and convergence. Riemann is better on smooth functions (order 2 against 1.5 at the midpoint), Lebesgue on functions with few values and on the unbounded case. The orders are governed by different things: smoothness of f in x for Riemann, smoothness of the distribution function t ↦ μ{f > t} for Lebesgue.

Efficiency. Riemann wins clearly, by two to three orders of magnitude in time. Computing a preimage requires looking at the domain, so a numerical Lebesgue sum always includes a domain search or a domain sample.

Different behaviour. Off-grid jumps (Lebesgue exact, Riemann O(1/N)), the singularity of 1/√x (Riemann left sum infinite, Lebesgue fine), infinitely many oscillations (root-finding Lebesgue stalls), the Dirichlet function (no Riemann integral, Lebesgue value 0), and Thomae's function (Riemann sums depend on the arithmetic of N, Lebesgue is exact at once).

Link to statistics. The layer cake is E[X] = ∫ P(X > t) dt, and L-sample is the empirical distribution function of f(U). Expectations, probabilities of events and limit theorems such as the LLN and CLT of Homework 3 are stated with Lebesgue integrals, because they need integrals over sets that are not intervals and the exchange of limits and integrals.

2 Numerical computation of the normal CDF

2.1 The problem

The standard normal density is φ(t) = e−t²/2/√(2π) and its CDF is Φ(x) = ∫−∞x φ(t) dt. By symmetry Φ(−x) = 1 − Φ(x) and Φ(0) = 1/2, so Φ(x) = 1/2 + ∫0x φ(t) dt. The normalising constant is known exactly: following Hogg, McKean and Craig (section 3.4), with I = ∫ e−z²/2 dz, I² = ∫∫ e−(y²+z²)/2 dy dz, and in polar coordinates I² = ∫02π∫0∞ e−r²/2 r dr dθ = 2π. So the integral over the whole line has a closed form, √(2π), while the integral up to an arbitrary x does not. The polar trick works only because the region is the whole plane.

2.2 Why Φ is not an elementary function: Liouville's theorem

Elementary functions are those built in finitely many steps from rational functions, exp, log and algebraic operations (roots), with composition; trigonometric functions are included through complex exponentials. Liouville (1835) proved a criterion for when an antiderivative stays in this class. The form needed here is:

If f and g are rational functions and g is not constant, then ∫ f(x)eg(x) dx is elementary if and only if there is a rational function R with f = R′ + R·g′. In that case ∫ f eg dx = R·eg.

For the normal density f = 1 and g = −x²/2, so we need a rational R with R′ − xR = 1. No such R exists:

(i) If R is a nonzero polynomial of degree d, then xR has degree d + 1 and R′ has degree d − 1, so R′ − xR has degree d + 1 ≥ 1 and cannot equal the constant 1. R = 0 gives 0 ≠ 1.

(ii) If R has a pole of order k ≥ 1 at some point a (possibly complex), then R′ has a pole of order k + 1 at a, while xR has a pole of order at most k. The pole of order k + 1 cannot cancel, so R′ − xR has a pole at a, but 1 has none.

Hence ∫ e−x²/2 dx is not elementary, and neither is Φ. A check of the criterion in a case where it succeeds: for ∫ x e−x²/2 dx we need R′ − xR = x, and R = −1 works, giving −e−x²/2, the familiar result. The result is about antiderivatives in closed form, not about computability: Φ is perfectly computable, it just cannot be written with finitely many elementary operations. The general algorithmic version of this theory is the Risch algorithm used in computer algebra systems.

2.3 The error function

Since the antiderivative cannot be expressed in old functions, it is given a name of its own:

erf(z) = (2/√π) ∫0z e−s² ds, erfc(z) = 1 − erf(z)

Substituting t = √2·s in ∫0x φ(t) dt gives the exact representation

Φ(x) = ½ [1 + erf(x/√2)], 1 − Φ(x) = ½ erfc(x/√2)

This is an exact analytical formula, and erf has known properties: it is odd, erf(∞) = 1, erf′(z) = (2/√π)e−z², it has a power series, an asymptotic expansion and a continued fraction. All of this is useful for proving things and for deriving algorithms, but a name does not give digits. erf is defined by an integral, and to get a number one of the methods below must still be run. The same is true of sin or exp, the difference is that for erf there is no finite elementary expression to fall back on. Libraries ship a dedicated implementation (typically rational approximations of the type published by W. J. Cody in 1969); JavaScript does not have Math.erf at all, which is why Homework 3 needed its own ncdf().

The thresholds come from a depth study of the continued fraction against the reference. At x = −2.5 depth 40 gives relative error 1.4·10−12 and depth 80 gives 1.4·10−16; at x = −4 depth 20 gives 8.0·10−13 and depth 40 is exact to rounding; from x = −6 depth 20 is already at 2·10−16. At x = −1 even depth 200 leaves 2.9·10−12, so near the centre the series is the right tool. The series for x < −2.5 loses relative accuracy because it returns ½ − (something close to ½): 8.9·10−14 at x = −3, 1.7·10−7 at −6, 0.16 at −8.

2.5 Comparison with a reference

The reference values were computed with the arbitrary-precision library mpmath (Python) at 40 significant digits and agree with the normal table in Hogg, McKean and Craig to the four printed decimals (for example Φ(1.645) = 0.9500, Φ(1.96) = 0.9750, Φ(2.576) = 0.9950). The table gives the relative error |estimate − Φ(x)| / Φ(x) of each method; 0 means the double-precision result is identical to the correctly rounded reference.

Away from the tails every method is usable: at x = 1.96 the errors are 7.1·10−8 (A&S), 3.8·10−6 (trapezoid), 8.1·10−11 (Simpson) and at rounding level for the series, the continued fraction and the hybrid. The left tail separates them. At x = −8, where Φ = 6.22·10−16, A&S is 1.8% off, the trapezoid 46%, Simpson 55%, the positive series 16%, and the erf Taylor series returns a number 1011 times too large, while the continued fraction and the hybrid are at 1.6·10−16. Every method that computes Φ as ½ plus or minus an integral loses the tail completely, because ½ − 0.4999999999999994 has only one or two correct digits left in double precision.

Numerical integration. The trapezoid error falls by 4 and Simpson's by 16 when n doubles (at x = 1.96: 2.08·10−6, 1.23·10−7, 7.57·10−9 for n = 8, 16, 32), the expected orders 2 and 4. Simpson needs n = 64 for 5·10−10 and n = 1024 for 7·10−15, i.e. a thousand evaluations of exp for one value of Φ. With n = 100 it costs about 1000 ns per value, 30 times A&S, and is still less accurate in absolute terms than the series.

Series. The erf Taylor series shows the cancellation directly:

The number of lost digits is about log10 of the largest term: at x = 6 the largest term is 8.4·105 and the absolute error is 4·10−11, at x = 8 the largest term is 5.6·1011 and the error 6.8·10−5, while the terms needed grow from 16 at x = 1 to 117 at x = 8. The positive series avoids this and has a maximum absolute error of 6.7·10−16, but it cannot give relative accuracy in the far left tail.

A&S 7.1.26. The fastest method (32 ns), with a maximum absolute error of 6.95·10−8, in line with half of the 1.5·10−7 bound on erf. That was more than enough for Homework 3, where it was used for KS distances of order 10−2, but the relative error reaches 3.3% in the tail, so it should not be used for small p-values.

Hybrid. Maximum absolute error 4.4·10−16 and maximum relative error 3.8·10−14 over the whole range (the worst points are just inside the series region near x = −2.4), at 177 ns per value: about five times slower than A&S, five times faster than Simpson with n = 100, and the only method that is accurate both in absolute and relative terms. The general lesson is the one from chapter 1: pick the representation to suit the region. Near the centre a series in x, in the tail an expansion around infinity, and never compute a small number as the difference of two numbers close to ½.

4 Conclusions

Riemann and Lebesgue sums converge to the same value for every Riemann-integrable function tested. Numerically the Riemann midpoint sum is cheaper and more accurate on smooth functions, with order 2 against 1.5, because a flat extremum of f becomes a square-root singularity of its distribution function. The Lebesgue partition is exact on step functions whatever the grid, handles the unbounded 1/√x better, and gives the Dirichlet and Thomae integrals at once, where Riemann sums either do not converge or depend on the arithmetic of N. Every numerical preimage measure still comes from the domain, and with sorted samples the Lebesgue sum is literally a reordered Riemann sum.

The normal CDF has no elementary antiderivative, by Liouville's theorem, because R′ − xR = 1 has no rational solution. The error function gives an exact representation, Φ(x) = ½[1 + erf(x/√2)], but its values still have to be computed. Quadrature, the Taylor series of erf and A&S 7.1.26 are all accurate in absolute terms near the centre and fail in relative terms in the tail. A hybrid of a positive series and the Laplace continued fraction gives full double precision on [−8, 8] at about 180 ns per evaluation.
